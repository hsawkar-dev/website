Prostate volume calculator

The calculator

Enter the three dimensions in any order. Most reports list them in centimeters; if yours shows millimeters (numbers like 45 × 40 × 35), switch the units.

The result is an estimate. Imaging software rounds the same way, and two scans of the same gland routinely differ by a few cc. Nothing here replaces an evaluation.

How the formula works

Imaging treats the prostate as an ellipsoid, a slightly flattened egg, and measures it along three axes. Volume is the product of the three, times a constant:

Volume (cc) = length × width × height × 0.52

The 0.52 is π divided by 6, rounded. Some software uses 0.523 or 0.5236; the difference is a fraction of a cc. Because the gland is not a perfect ellipsoid, the formula is a close estimate rather than an exact measurement.

A worked example: a report reading 4.5 × 4.0 × 3.5 cm gives 4.5 × 4.0 × 3.5 × 0.52 = 32.8, so about 33 cc, mild enlargement.

What your volume means

cc and grams are interchangeable here, because prostate tissue weighs almost exactly what water does. Size decides which treatments fit; symptoms decide whether to treat at all.

PSA density: why volume changes what PSA means

PSA comes from all prostate tissue, benign or not, so a large gland can produce an elevated PSA with no cancer anywhere. Dividing PSA by volume, a figure called PSA density, adjusts for that. A PSA of 6 means something different in a 30 cc prostate (density 0.20) than in a 90 cc prostate (density 0.07).

Many urologists use 0.15 ng/mL per cc as the level above which a closer look, usually an MRI, is considered. Density is one input alongside your exam, your PSA trend over time, and imaging; it is not a diagnosis. Typical PSA values by age are on the PSA levels by age chart.

Common questions about prostate volume

What is the formula for prostate volume

Length times width times height times 0.52. It is the formula for the volume of an ellipsoid, and 0.52 is pi divided by 6, rounded. Ultrasound machines and MRI software use the same formula, so entering the three dimensions from your report reproduces the volume they would print.

Is prostate volume in cc the same as grams

For practical purposes, yes. Prostate tissue is almost exactly the density of water, so a 60 cc prostate weighs about 60 grams. Ultrasound and MRI reports usually say cc or mL; pathology reports say grams.

What is a normal prostate volume

About 20 to 30 cc for a healthy adult, roughly walnut-sized. Reports often call anything over 30 cc enlarged and over 40 cc clearly enlarged, but those are conventions. A 45 cc prostate at 70 is ordinary; whether it blocks flow is the real question.

Why do my ultrasound and MRI give different volumes

The gland is not a perfect ellipsoid, and each scan measures its edges slightly differently, so two scans of the same prostate routinely differ by a few cc. Ultrasound through the belly is the least precise; transrectal ultrasound and MRI measure more closely. A small difference does not change the treatment conversation.

How do I calculate PSA density

Divide your PSA in ng/mL by your prostate volume in cc. A PSA of 6 with a 60 cc prostate gives a density of 0.10. Many urologists use 0.15 as the level above which a closer look, usually an MRI, is considered. Density is one input alongside your exam, PSA trend, and MRI, not a diagnosis.
